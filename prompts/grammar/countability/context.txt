Classify how the supplied noun sense is used grammatically in English. Countability is language-specific, even when the underlying concept is shared across languages.

- countable: this sense normally combines directly with a numeral or an indefinite article, as in "one book" and "two books".
- uncountable: this sense is normally used as a mass noun; direct numeral use needs a unit, portion, kind, or changed sense, as with "some furniture" versus "two pieces of furniture".
- both: this specific sense genuinely has ordinary count and mass constructions without changing the referent or switching to a separately represented sense. Do not use both merely because the headword has another sense elsewhere.

Judge the supplied sense and its ordinary usage. For example, chicken meaning the animal is countable (one chicken, two chickens), while chicken meaning the meat is uncountable; these are different lemmas, so neither is both on that basis. Do not infer countability merely from whether physical instances exist: furniture has pieces but English "furniture" is uncountable. Do not infer whether a noun has singular and plural forms; exceptional number paradigms belong to number_type. Record the usual classification explicitly, including countable.
